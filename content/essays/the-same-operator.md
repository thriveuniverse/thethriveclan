---
title: The Same Operator
teaser: Other people saw the pattern before I did. It took a piece of software running thirty decision cycles to show me what they were looking at.
---

I chose maths at A-level because it was easy. Not because I had a plan. Not because I saw a future in it. Because of all the subjects available, maths was the one where I could sit in a lesson, understand what was happening, and know that understanding was enough. If I understood something, I didn't need to study it. The answer was always already there in the mess — you just had to find the diagram hiding inside it.

I was lazy. Maths let me be lazy in a way that still worked.

So I went to university to do more of it. Three years of lectures I attended religiously, tutorials I mostly skipped, coursework I barely handed in. I passed, not brilliantly - but I passed. Not because I worked hard but because I understood, and understanding was the work.

It never occurred to me to ask what came after.

There was no plan to control what came next. Just the same instinct, doing what it always did, trusting it would hold up against whatever showed up.

---

What came after arrived the way most things in my life have arrived. Not through a plan. Through a gap, and someone who thought I might fit it.

I wrote to a friend of my late father and said, honestly, that I had absolutely no idea what to do with my life but that his industry sounded interesting and would it be possible to start at the bottom and see if anything fitted. He interviewed me. Called in the head of trading. My financial market knowledge was minimal and I said so. They took me on anyway.

Years later I was bored and called a headhunter who had known my father. Ray Wallhead. A headhunter with four decades of experience in the Square Mile, regarded as something close to a god — not because he placed people in jobs but because he could see people in a way most couldn't, and he was almost never wrong. I explained I was bored and asked if he thought he could find me something. He agreed to take me on.

He found me a role as head of shipping and administration at a commodity trading company. I had never seen a bill of lading. I said so in the interview. The MD said he hadn't expected to be interviewing me — Ray had insisted I was the right person, so here we both were. We had a great conversation. The person retiring from the role had been there forty years. Ray believed the company needed someone who could see the system without being inside it, and Ray was convinced he knew what I could do.

I got the job because Ray saw something. Not because I had presented myself cleverly. Because someone with a rare gift for reading people had read me, and staked his reputation on what he found.

---

I have done a personality test. INFJ-T, if you want the label. It wasn't wrong. But it wasn't particularly revealing either. What surprised me about Last Prompt wasn't that it described me. It was that it identified a recurring behavioural pattern and a recurring bill.

I would describe myself, if pressed, as a kind of people person — but not in the usual way. Not in the sense of thriving in crowds or filling a room with energy. More that when I face a problem, my instinct is to solve it through people rather than around them — and usually by building something new rather than patching what's already there. Trying to pull people in to my idea rather than instructing. A new system rather than a workaround. Transparency rather than directives. Helping people become part of the solution rather than simply pointing them toward it. The instinct is generative. Which is also, it turns out, where the invoice tends to arrive.

I knew that about myself in a vague way. The way you know something without quite being able to see it - or define it.

What I didn't know — what took a piece of software and thirty decision cycles to show me — was how consistent it was. And what it cost.

---

When I ran the Colony in Last Prompt, I wasn't playing for cohesion. I was just solving the problems as they arrived. The stranger at the gate. The rationing dispute. The winter infrastructure. I solved them the way I solve problems. Community leaders. Volunteers. Transparency. Mediation. By the end of the chapter, cohesion was at ten. Infrastructure was quietly paying the bill.

When I ran Corporate Reckoning, the vocabulary changed entirely. Secondments instead of volunteers. Internal mobility instead of community leaders. Reskilling instead of education. The language of the corporate world rather than the post-collapse settlement.

The pattern was identical.

The AI evaluating my decisions across two completely different environments, two different worlds, two different sets of pressures, came back with the same observation across both.

Different vocabulary. The same operator.

And then one phrase that stopped me:

*Miranda frequently builds humanly elegant solutions and occasionally forgets the invoice.*

That is the most accurate description of my life, not just professional, that I have ever read. More accurate than the personality test. More accurate than anything I would have said about myself. Produced by something that had never met me, reading over thirty decision cycles in two fictional worlds.

---

I thought about Ray when I read it.

Ray didn't need thirty decision cycles. He had forty years of reading people and a gift that couldn't really be explained, only trusted. He looked at me — at someone who had fallen into maths because it was easy, written a letter saying she had no idea what she wanted, shown up to an interview not knowing what a bill of lading was — and saw an operator. Saw the pattern. Staked his reputation on it.

I didn't see it. I was the turbulent 71% — quietly getting things done while internally uncertain whether I was doing it right. I was not playing to my strengths. I was being myself, which is a different thing. Playing to your strengths implies you know what they are. I didn't. I just kept solving problems the only way I knew how and occasionally someone with the right eyes was in the room to see what that meant.

Ray was one of those people. Last Prompt turned out to be another.

---

The more useful thing Last Prompt showed me was not simply the pattern. Knowing I care about people was not news. The more useful thing was the predictable cost of caring about people this way.

The people solution is strong. The infrastructure bill arrives later.

That's not a flaw to eliminate. It's a pattern to watch. The goal isn't to become someone who builds less humanly elegant solutions — those solutions work, and they work because they reflect something real about how I see problems. The goal is to get better at remembering the invoice. To notice earlier when the glass is quietly paying for the marble. To give the operator I've always been a few fewer blind corners.

That is what I think Last Prompt actually does, at the level that matters most.

Not the scores. Not the evaluation. The mirror.

It shows you the shape of the person you've always been — not in the language of a personality type or a competency framework, but in the specific, observable, data-produced record of how you actually behave when the pressure is real and nobody is watching you perform a version of yourself. Thirty decisions. Two worlds. One operator.

And if you know that operator clearly enough — really know them, in the way the data shows rather than the way you narrate yourself — then whatever the environment becomes, whatever changes, whatever the future does or doesn't cooperate with, you have the one thing that transfers.

Not a profession. Not a credential. Not a plan.

Yourself, clearly seen. With fewer blind corners.

The purpose of reasoning is not to control the future. It is to remain viable when the future refuses to cooperate.

I have been doing that all along. I just didn't have the words for it until someone else supplied them — first Ray, in the Square Mile, and then a piece of software in a post-collapse colony, reading the same thing he read.

There she goes again.
